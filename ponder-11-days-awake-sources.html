Sources

The eleven days, as recorded at the time
  1. 1
    Neurological findings after prolonged sleep deprivationArchives of Neurology, 1965 (Ross). The navy doctor's own report on the 264 hours. It has no online abstract; its day-by-day notes are summarised in the Psychiatric Times article below. Supports the video: the day-by-day changes (trouble focusing his eyes and recognising objects by touch on day 2; moodiness and failed tongue twisters on day 3; a street sign seen as a person and the football-player delusion on day 4; the forest path on day 5; slowed speech and trouble naming objects on day 6; unfinished sentences by day 9; suspicion of a radio host on day 10; the counting test that stopped at 65 on day 11).
  2. 2
    Psychiatric and EEG observations on a case of prolonged (264 hours) wakefulnessArchives of General Psychiatry, 1966 (Gulevich, Dement and Johnson). The original clinical report of the case, including the sleep recordings made afterwards. It has no online abstract; the video relies on it through the later summaries listed here (264 hours, changed sleep on the recovery nights, no lasting psychiatric harm).
  3. 3
    Sleep deprivation, psychosis and mental efficiencyPsychiatric Times (Coren). A readable summary of the doctor's day-by-day notes and of the pinball game on day 10. Supports the video: the order of the symptoms and the contrast between his failing speech and his pinball win.
His own account, decades later
  1. 4
    Lessons from sleeplessness: The 60th anniversary of Randy Gardner's world recordNPR, 2024. Supports the video: the science fair project in San Diego, the coin toss, the 260-hour record he set out to beat, the nausea on day three, the sleep researcher driving down to meet him, his short fuse with reporters on day 11, about 14 hours of sleep in the naval hospital, feeling no groggier than a normal person, the later insomnia (five or six hours in bed for about 15 minutes of sleep), and the phrase karmic payback.
  2. 5
    What is the limit to how long a human can stay awake? And why we do not monitor the recordGuinness World Records, 2023. Supports the video: loud music and cold showers with no stimulant drugs, late-night basketball and winning every pinball game, the researcher's statement that no psychiatric problems followed, his later acceptance that brief microsleeps probably happened, the later record of 453 hours 40 minutes (almost 19 days), the decision to stop monitoring the category because of the danger, the insomnia easing after about a decade, and the phrase karmic payback.
What a sleepless brain does
  1. 6
    Adenosine: a mediator of the sleep-inducing effects of prolonged wakefulnessScience, 1997 (Porkka-Heiskanen et al.). An animal study. Supports the video: a chemical signal builds up in the brain during long wakefulness and falls again during recovery sleep. The video calls it a chemical signal and does not name it.
  2. 7
    Local sleep in awake ratsNature, 2011 (Vyazovskiy et al.). Supports the video: after a long time awake, small groups of brain cells briefly go silent as in sleep while the animal stays awake and active, and performance slips when they do.
  3. 8
    Selective neuronal lapses precede human cognitive lapses following sleep deprivationNature Medicine, 2017 (Nir et al.). Supports the video: in sleep-deprived people, individual brain cells respond weakly and late just before a lapse, with sleep-like slow activity in that region.
  4. 9
    Sleep deprivation in the rat: an update of the 1989 paperSleep, 2002 (Rechtschaffen and Bergmann). Supports the video: rats kept from sleeping continuously died within weeks, and no single cause of death was established.

A note on what is shown

The young man in this video is a real person; only his first name is spoken, and he, his friends, the doctor and the scientist are drawn as simple stickmen that do not resemble real people. The bead string, the tube of teal liquid and the dam are visual metaphors, not anatomy. The idea that parts of his brain were napping was never measured in him: it comes from later studies in rats and in other people, and the video calls it the best explanation we have. The recovery-sleep details rest on the 1966 report as summarised in later sources. Nothing links his later insomnia to the experiment, and the video says so. Results in rats do not transfer directly to people. This video is for information only and is not medical advice. Do not try to go without sleep; if you have trouble sleeping, talk to a doctor.
